Here's a summary of this episode of "Neues Bauen" (New Building) — a podcast by Büro Hauser about transformation in the architecture industry.

Guest: Prof. Dr. Thomas Wortmann, Tenure Track Professor at the Institute for Computational Design and Construction (ICD), University of Stuttgart.

Topic: AI, data, and machine learning in architecture — cutting through the hype.

Key points:

Thomas Wortmann works at the intersection of computer science and architecture, a field he calls "architectural informatics" (Architekturinformatik) — a bridge discipline that's existed almost as long as computer science itself but is only now gaining dedicated study programs, mostly at the master's level. He argues it needs to become part of undergraduate architecture education too.

On practical applications today, he highlights parametric design with Rhino/Grasshopper as an entry point. Larger engineering firms and startups are already automating the design of rule-based buildings like parking garages, office buildings, and apartment blocks. Some startups position themselves as replacements for architects, working directly with developers. Optimization studies show that simply optimizing a building's geometry — slightly smaller windows, different orientation — can save around 10% energy, which he considers almost irresponsible to ignore.

Regarding image generation tools like Midjourney, he's cautious. These models tend toward clichés (e.g., "sustainable building" always shows trees on facades) and can't produce architecturally sound designs. He sees them as useful for indirect creative inspiration — generating abstract imagery that architects then interpret — rather than generating buildings directly.

On the future, he advocates for neurosymbolic AI: combining the creativity of neural networks with rule-based symbolic methods that understand building codes, structural integrity, and norms. Neural networks alone don't know right from wrong — they can't verify compliance with DIN standards or structural feasibility.

He references a Goldman Sachs study ranking architecture as the third or fourth most affected profession in terms of tasks that could be automated — roughly 30% of current tasks. Drawing and routine documentation work will decline, while higher-level cognitive tasks like quality review, optimization, and creative direction will grow. The challenge is that these new tasks aren't yet compensated under Germany's current fee structure (HOAI).

His call to action: the digital wave is already here. Germany lags behind — countries like Singapore have required BIM for all building permits for years, and Denmark now mandates life-cycle analysis with every building application. He urges architects, universities, and policymakers to embrace digitalization rather than deny the wave exists, and recommends Grasshopper as a practical first step for any practice wanting to get started.
