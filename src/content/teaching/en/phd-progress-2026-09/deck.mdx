<Slide>
  <div class="pp-head">
    <h3>Polyhedral meshes</h3>
    <h2>The node decides</h2>
  </div>
  <Valence />
  <SlideNotes>Triangles: valence 6, planar for free, but six beams per node, torsion at the nodes, and offsets fail. Quads: valence 4, planar quads follow conjugate curve networks, so planarity dictates the layout. Hexagons: valence 3, face offsets exist for free, three plates at a node: the case compas_timber already handles. Also on the list: quad-hex (PH-PQ), mixed and agent-based strategies that take FEM and fabricability as inputs, and origami.</SlideNotes>
</Slide>

---
text: |
  ### Why hexagons

  ## Three planes always meet in one point.

  # Six *almost never* do.
notes: "Offset here means a parallel mesh with the same combinatorics at constant distance: an inner and an outer layer. Move each face plane along its normal and re-intersect. At a valence-3 vertex, three offset planes still meet in exactly one point, always. At a valence-6 vertex, six planes would have to pass through one point: three fix it, the other three have no reason to hit it. A triangle mesh gets a vertex offset instead; face distance varies, edges twist, nodes stop being repeatable."
---

text: |
  ### The gap

  ## Many algorithms. *Little control.*

  - No reliable way to control how panel sizes are distributed
  - LLM to mesh algorithm to shape: still slow, and the design is poor
  - Missing: an encapsulated algorithm that transfers across software and languages, **with tests and checks**
notes: "There are already many panelling algorithms; usability and control are the barrier. An LLM cannot do this kind of algorithmic modelling right away. What seems missing is a framework in which LLMs handle algorithms reliably across software and languages. Tests and checks are the critical missing piece, which is the property-first skill again."

text: |
  ### Structure · 1 of 4 · The promise

  ## If the diagram closes, *it stands*

  3D graphic statics: equilibrium only, no stiffness, no simulation. A free structural filter?
notes: "Yost et al. 2022, Akbarzadeh's polyhedral line, Schneider supplying the glass. Equilibrium only. If it worked, it would be a structural filter at almost no cost."
---

---
text: |
  ### Structure · 2 of 4 · Killed as proposed

  ## On our 12 m roof, plates are *1 to 4 %* utilised

  Deflection governs, so an equilibrium check on plate capacity rejects nothing. And wind lifts a light roof: compression-only reports "no solution" for a roof that is fine.
notes: "Our own geometry, 12 m span, 140 mm plates. The idea as proposed dies on both ends: it rejects nothing, and it rejects the wrong thing under uplift."
---

---
text: |
  ### Structure · 3 of 4 · The joint

  ## The plate is not the structure. *The joint is.*

  Edge capacity of 18 to 45 kN/m against a demand of 11 to 46 kN/m. The fold turns part of the force across the grain, the most brittle direction wood has.
notes: "Force arrives at the edges. A glued or screwed edge is the same order as the demand (design tension from DIBt Z-9.1-917 with k_mod 0.10). The fold decides what the joint carries. And one 3D resultant per edge is exactly what an equilibrium method returns for free. So graphic statics survives, but as a joint check, not a plate check."

text: |
  ### Why cheap changes the question

  ## At FEA prices you check three load cases.

  # Nearly free, you check *the whole envelope*.

  Asymmetric snow, wind uplift, and: remove one plate, does an admissible state still exist?
notes: "Characterising the admissible region instead of sampling it, in a form an engineer recognises. What could still kill it: the origin of k_mod 0.10 (DIBt's ruling or TS3's own tests) decides whether tier 1 binds. Glued edges are brittle, so a valid glued check needs the force distribution along the edge, which rigid-block methods do not return. Screwed is defensible today. Nobody else does equilibrium methods on plate assemblies with distributed edge joints: IBOIS uses semi-rigid springs and FEA, Leeds uses CRA on a small dry-jointed model, Stuttgart uses a minimum edge length as the design rule."

    <h2>Acoustics and fabrication belong <em>in the plate</em></h2>

      <p>CNC is exact. On-site assembly is not.</p>
      <ul>
        <li>Scan, detect, regenerate the next batch, so errors do not accumulate</li>
        <li>Assembly templates that leave a slack</li>
        <li>Scaffolding, cranes, self-locking designs with 3D graphic statics</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
